Payments Ops Analyst

An AI assistant that explains why card approval rates changed, and shows the numbers and documents behind its answer.

Where the project stands: 20 of 22 work items are done, and Sprint 6 runs through November 9, 2026. See the project board or read the scope and timeline.

The problem

Every time someone pays by card, the payment is approved or declined. Payment companies track the approval rate, the share of payments that go through, because every drop means more declines. Some are legitimate, but some are good customers turned away: a lost sale and an unhappy customer.

When that rate drops, someone has to find out why. It might be a bank that has gone down, a slow network or a new fraud rule. Answering usually means two separate jobs: digging through reports to see what changed, then searching change logs and notes to find what caused it.

This project does both jobs. You ask a question in plain English, and an assistant built on Claude answers from the reports and the documents, then says where each fact came from.

How it works

1. Build the reportsA data pipeline cleans 2 million simulated card payments and summarizes them into reports: approval rates, decline reasons, response times and disputes.
2. Make the documents searchableTen made-up operations documents, such as change logs and how-to guides, are split into 52 sections and indexed by meaning. A search for "declined electronics orders" can find a rule change that uses different words.
3. Let Claude use bothClaude gets six read-only tools: five that query the reports and one that searches the documents. It can look things up, but it can't change anything.

The numbers say what happened, and the documents suggest why. The assistant is told to treat a matching document as a lead, then check that its dates and scope fit the numbers.

An example

The payment data here is simulated, and it contains a few problems I planted on purpose. One of them was a fraud rule that went too far. This is what happened when I asked about that week without mentioning fraud or any type of merchant.

The question, shortened Approval rates looked off during the week of September 21. What moved, why did it happen, and what can't you confirm?

Here is what the assistant found:

What changed, from the reportsOnline electronics purchases declined as suspected fraud went from about 8 a day to 666, 678 and 650 on September 22, 23 and 24, then back to 10. Across all online payments, approvals fell from 91.66% to 88.89%.
Why, from the documentsA fraud rule change log records a stricter rule for online electronics that started on September 22, and a rollback on September 25 after merchants complained. The team had expected about 1.5 times the normal declines.

It also listed what it couldn't confirm. For example, the data doesn't show whether the declined customers were genuine, so it wouldn't claim they were.

I then checked the answer against the source data. The figures matched, with one exception: it said declines reached "about 80 times" normal, and the actual figure is about 86. It also said it couldn't show the rise was spread across banks, when the data could have shown that. Neither mistake changed its conclusion. Read the full answer and the check.

Where this fits in a card payment

Card payments typically run on the four-party model. The cardholder uses a card from their bank, the issuer. The merchant accepts the card through its bank, the acquirer. A card network connects the two banks, and its switch routes each authorization request to the issuer and carries the answer back.

CardholderPays by card. In the data: card token, wallet flag and channel.
MerchantStarts the request. In the data: merchant, category and region.
AcquirerThe merchant's bank. Not in the data.
Network switchRoutes the request. Not in the data as its own step.
IssuerApproves or declines. In the data: the bank and the response code.

Solid boxes are in the data and dashed boxes are not. The answer returns along the same route, usually within seconds. The money moves later, through clearing and settlement, which are also not simulated. Latency is recorded as one total for each request.

Whose view this is. The reports and documents are written from the viewpoint of an operations team at a fictional payment processor, a company that handles authorizations on behalf of many banks and merchants. That position is why it can compare patterns across 40 banks at once, and why the three planted problems sit in different places:

  • Bank outage: at the issuer. The bank's system stops answering, so requests fail with code 91.
  • UK slowdown: at the processor. A setting change on its link to the card network makes requests queue, so they're slow but still complete.
  • Strict fraud rule: at the processor. Its own rule declines payments at every bank, which is why the rise shows up across all 40 banks and not just one.

What this doesn't prove

  • The test is easier than real life. I wrote the data, the documents and the test questions, so I knew the answers. Real change logs are messier and less complete.
  • The sample is small. The evaluation is 12 runs across 4 questions. That shows the assistant's answers vary from run to run, but it isn't a large test.
  • Nothing alerts anyone yet. A table flags unusual changes. It caught all three planted problems, though its thresholds were set knowing the answers. On four new ones it caught 2 of 3, missed 1 by 2.5 declines, and raised no false alarms. That is a small test on the same simulated data. There's no dashboard or alert, so a person still has to look. That's the work still to do.
  • Search results can be close. In one test, the right document won by 0.620 to 0.617, and a good result doesn't always put the most useful section first.
  • The payment flow is simplified. The data covers the cardholder, merchant, issuer and disputes. It leaves out the acquirer, the network as a separate step, and clearing and settlement. The decline codes follow general ISO 8583 style, while each real network and processor has its own list, so the code numbers here won't match any one network exactly.
  • There's no live demo. It runs on my own Databricks and Claude accounts. You can read the sample answers, or run the code and its tests yourself.

What I learned

  • Averages hide problems. The bank outage didn't show in daily totals by region. It only appeared after filtering to one decline reason and ranking by bank and day.
  • Correct counts can hide wrong content. The first document table had exactly the right 52 rows, yet every row held a whole document instead of one section. Reading the actual text caught it, and the pipeline now fails if that happens again.
  • An AI's answer still needs checking. The assistant always found the right cause when pointed at a problem, yet 2 of those 9 answers misstated a figure, once while its own query showed the right number.

Technical details

PartBuilt with
Data pipelinePySpark and Spark Declarative Pipelines (Lakeflow) on Databricks Free Edition, serverless, deployed with a Databricks bundle into Unity Catalog. Bronze, silver and gold layers, with Auto Loader ingestion and data quality expectations.
Document search (RAG)Markdown split at headings into a table, a Databricks Vector Search Delta Sync index, and databricks-gte-large-en embeddings.
AssistantA Python server using the Model Context Protocol (MCP), used from Claude Code. The report tools run on in-memory DuckDB with file and network access off, and the SQL tool accepts only a single SELECT.
Testing17 pytest tests that run without Databricks, including a fake document search, plus a separate script that checks the live search. A runner starts each evaluation session in an empty folder with only the assistant's tools, so it can't read the answers in this repo.

Every result above has its evidence listed in the project summary.
